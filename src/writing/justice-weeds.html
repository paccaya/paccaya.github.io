Three-body intimation

There is a certain work of dualities (Good versus Bad, ugly versus beautiful, or the rational versus irrational) to hide their own mutual entanglement. One can only think of one relative to their opposite.

One can only conceive of what is "beautiful" in relation to something "ugly."
One can only consider what is "good" relative to what is "bad."
"One" can only be conceived when there is "two."

One can think of "nature" only when we have considered the "artificial."

And we can only be familiar with their deeper relationship when a third body enters the chat.

An intervention. A mediation. Usually something lurking in the depths that is shrouded in itself can only be seen by one well-acquainted with darkness.

How we twitch when the banners of "artificial intelligence" stream as new flagship models drop. As in their queries and promptings of safety, utility, and thus, disruption, they bring us to the frightening plains of how "artificial" can be combined and enmeshed with the word "intelligence." And there is no mediator for this chat, at least not in the forebodings of these times. It is as though we are in the eye of a storm, the surroundings being fear of AI and impending doom, despair, and collapse. Of all those of its adherents who warn of it.

As with every fear there is a longing to weed. We don't want to be afraid. We want to conquer things of threat. Remove those bugs, glitches, and weeds from the capstones of consciousness. And in its removal there is a naming to things to be "unwanted"—a dis-owning of things. In the naming, the reminding memory of how there are things that should not be here. In the mirroring of that, one could say there are many things that are now longing to be removed in the introduction of AI.

AI should be here to speed things up and support efficiency and streamlining.

Though, AI should not be there where artists work: in poetry, song, and in the cradles of creation. AI should not be doing the homework of children. AI should support us, not allow us to avoid things we need to do.

To opt out of the moral imperatives that only speak to discomfort and fear (conclusions of Good and Bad), I want to address some deceptions. There is a narrowed frame of expression on AI, it takes these forms

In this instance, is AI Good?
Does this help our business?
How is AI disrupting this?
Is AI safe?

The work of deception and obscurity is to narrow apertures to their deepest closing. Most notably because in closing, there is not much openness.

To open something is to scandalized all that has been closed. Closed enough that any door ajar may be wildly controversial is what these questions are actually querying can be reflections on ourselves. Puddles that we jump back in shock from seeing our reflection distorted. Because that is what a second body does: a second body is a mirror. A second body allows an intimation with the other-body. Since a second body can only be established in contrast to something Other. The Old English oþer meaning "second" or "additional," thus something different enough to be distinguished, then able to be separated.

A third body then brings into deep intimacy all that is then entangled the first two bodies: of knowing the unknown, ascertaining the uncertain, and provoking the unwanted.

It is of "artificial intelligence" to open these questions. In the same ways that insoluble questions are questions asked in the form of answers, these questions about AI are incomplete in the way that they pretend to be complete. A transition to the second body is needed in order to shed its first. Though that transition can only be vividly expressed on a boat. The third body is the one that allows one to float rather than drown in the seduction of the first two bodies.

AI was the second body. We were the first. Who else could join the party?

There is an art of dualities (first bodies and second bodies) to mask third bodies. Since the monogamy of the first and second body is utterly so romantic. Two is a pair. Three is a bit of scare.

Third bodies are conceived as disrupting normal and of needing remediation to dilate things back to the mean.

Third bodies often come with names: troublemakers, the mad, unruly, disordered, and people who need help.

That is why justice is in the weeds. It is in the places that we do not see because weeds are a nuisance. Weed has meaning. The surface intransitive it is "to remove what is troublesome or undesirable.” Though in its depth there is depth. The Kentish Old English wied means "grass, herb, and weed." It is a troublesome act in its defining and roots for a word to mean a word. Furthermore troublesome when the word is dually syncopated. Simply put, when a word can mean two things at once in different temporalities, trouble comes to meaning.

That is why justice is in the weeds.

Weeds can be two things at once.

Justice can be two things at once.

Things can be two things at once.

This is troubling because one's Justice can be another's Oppression. Troubling also because language conveniently hides its entanglement. People have gone over this yet this is exactly still why Justice has been backdoored by weeds.

Because we want to remove the "two-things-at-once" into categories: it is convenient to say you are this, so they must be that.

I am me so that you are you.

They are the aggressor and you are the victim.

They are responsible, so you are not.

They are the one who is hurt, so you must not be.

They are the ones who do not know what you know, so they are ____.

The three-body intimation can be found in Loretta J. Ross' Calling In: How to Start to Making Change with Those You’d Rather Cancel where she wrote about a first-hand account of how justice is in the weeds:

1. How I Learned to Call In

I know the world is bruised and bleeding, and though it is important not to ignore its pain, it is also critical to refuse to its malevolence.
—Toni Morrison

When the iron gate slammed shut behind me, I shuffled forward and held out my arms. The two male male prison guards reached out and touched too much, fondling me as they searched for contraband. They seemed to relish it. I tried to block them out, to retreat into a little corner in my mind. I didn’t even want to breathe until it was over.

A gruff voice, a buzz, then another set of bars swung open, and I stepped through. When the last gate clanged, I was surrounded by stark concrete walls.

I was locked in now, about to encounter a man who was someone’s nightmare. Who might yet be mine?

Lorton Reformatory housed the men who’d been convicted of the worst crimes in the nation’s capital. It was 1979 and I was twenty-six. I was the newly minted director of the Washington, DC Rape Crisis Center, and I had driven an hour to the notorious Northern Virginia prison on an unusual misson. I had come to talk to a man who was serving a life sentence for raping and murdering a woman. I had to come to talk to him about why not to rape. One-on-one, face-to-face.

On the way in, the prison had looked like an idyllic college campus—vibrant green quads, trim black buildings, perfect archways, and curved walkways—until you saw the watchtower, armed with guards and rifles, and barbed wire. Until you’d felt the guards’ touch. Until you heard the clang.

But now—What the f*!# am I doing here? I felt scared and unprepared. I had no particular qualifications for this visit, and I could no longer tell you what I’d been thinking when I’d agreed to it.

The man I’d arranged to see was named William Fuller. He had written to the Center months earlier, asking for help. I can still remember the crux of his letter, more than forty years later: “Outside I raped women; inside I rape men. I’d like not to be a rapist anymore.” He was serving a life sentence for raping and murdering a Black school teacher named Ethel Dorsey. He had been incarcerated for fifteen years by that time.

Fuller’s letter sparked passionate disagreement at the Center. Our organization had been around since 1972, when it launched as the first rape crisis center in the country. But it still ran understaffed on a threadbare budget. Most of us were offended by the letter. We didn’t have enough resources to help victims. How dare a rapist seek help from us? Some employees thought the letter was a setup; maybe Fuller was trying to manipulate us into becoming mules to smuggle contraband. Others thought it might be a play to drum up illicit conjugal visits. Or perhaps it was a long play, to get the Center to support Parole application.

My first response was outright anger, followed closely by disgust. I wanted to send this man my own outraged letter, calling him for even thinking we would talk to him. Once I’d read his note, I didn’t want to touch it, as if the paper itself was contaminated.

Yet I couldn’t put the letter out of my mind. And tellingly, I hadn’t thrown it into the trash. The letter sat on my desk for months, lurking beneath other paperwork but never forgotten. It was like a loose tooth I couldn’t stop wiggling.

Eventually, my curiosity won. Over the objections of some of the other staffers, I wrote back, offering a counseling session. We had never worked with perpetrators before, only rape victims. We offered all manner of support, and it was important work, but something was frustrating me. Our work wasn’t stopping any rapists from raping. And I wanted to understand the mind of a rapist, a man from my Black community who chose to brutalize women like me.

It was only as I stood frozen—in that cramped drab hallway in Lorton Reformatory—that I realized how poorly this could go. I had no plan, no script. And I had no special qualifications, except for one thing: I was a survivor of sexual assault. Those scars were still bleeding all over my life.

What in the world could a rape survivor say to a convicted rapist? What would I say, when I walked through the door at the end of the hall and faced him?

Loretta J. Ross brings up a salient point, as it hurts to touch its angle when so pointed outward: what if those who acted out violence wanted healing? What if outwards (to places banished, forbidden, and taboo) is the nursing ward for hospicing modernity? What if justice is in the weeds, and it’s not so much the weeds that we pull that support the garden but in the recycling of their matter? The reintegration of their nutrient through their decomposition? To not use a metaphor: what if justice is not in eradicating the villain, the plotter, and the actor rather than the acted upon, but in extinguishing the idea that bad people are to be called out to then face the consequences?

Someone once said: a murder is never the first murder. I am comfortable saying that we tend to underestimate the hurt that is behind those that carry out harm. Which is why justice is in the weeds. We can pull out what we don’t want, yet if the weeds do not return to the soil, then the nutrients of pain: in sorrow, grief, and careful tending to resentment do not return and re-integrate. Thus, the self lives out still in fear of Others. Those different to us. Those so out of our circle of care they are more of an Saturnic ringed orbit to an inner home. Loretta J. Ross observed this:

When I went through that final locked door in Lorton and entered the prison meeting room, six men awaited me. I’d been expecting one man, Fuller, and even he I didn’t know how to picture. I was taken aback. It wasn’t just how many there were; it was how big they were. I didn’t anticipate that they would be so physically intimidating, like buffed-up MMA fighters.

Only years later did I realize that I was meeting the prison’s predators: the men who bulked their bodies up to avoid becoming prey.

They were sitting in a circle, calmly dressed in civilian clothes instead of prison jumpsuits. I didn’t know what to say beyond “Hi.” I felt supremely out of place. But one man spoke up and took the lead. He was William Fuller, he said, and he introduced the man sitting next to him as Larry Cannon. The pair had started the group that was now sitting before me, which they called Prisoners Against Rape.

Fuller didn’t seem alarmed at my wide-eyed confusion. He just kept talking, introducing me to the group and its mission. He spoke about “rape culture” and how they wanted to change how they viewed women. I’d heard the term “rape culture” before, but never from the mouths of Black men and never from men convicted of sexual assault. I began to realize that they had been changing themselves long before they reached out to the Center. I exhaled for what felt like the first time since I’d entered the prison.

At first, they only shared their experiences in broad terms like “I mistreated women” or “I was convicted of raping women.” I didn’t question their vagueness. After all, people rarely perceive themselves as villains in their own stories, and abusers frequently minimize what they’ve done. Besides, I didn’t want the graphic details.

After those who wanted to speak had introduced themselves, Fuller asked if I was willing to help them learn about things like feminism, Black history, and violence against women. I think I’d already decided to work with them. However, I felt it was important to spell out my nonnegotiable rules. I told them that I would not be writing letters to parole boards on their behalf. I would not be the slightest bit patient with any romantic overtures. And I would not be bringing in anything from outside—no drugs or clothes or cigarettes. Only books. They all nodded in agreement. I couldn’t detect any disappointment in their faces, but perhaps they were good at hiding their reactions. And so, I began teaching Black feminist theory to rapists, my first calling in project. I instinctively approached their wrongdoing with accountability and education, hoping that they could change if given a chance. I didn’t have a name for it then; I just knew I had to try to make a difference.

In the early sessions, I tried to hide my fear. I didn’t precisely feel unsafe, because there were alert prison guards just outside the door. But I did not want to appear hypervigilant as a result of my PTSD, constantly expecting to be raped again. I took comfort in knowing these men were never getting out; they were serving life sentences. I tried to remember that I could come and go, while they could not. But I also didn’t feel relaxed.

At the Center, we often began with our truths. So, in that first session, after setting my guidelines, I fell back on the only real thing I could be sure of: my own story. That’s what we did at the Crisis Center: telling our stories, hoping to be believed, hoping we could change how women were treated. I shared the story of the incest that led to my pregnancy; how angry I was about what happened to me; how I’d had to overcome my anger and fear to meet with them. I quipped that working at the Center meant that we risked getting a skewed perception of men. Even though I was fortunate to have five brothers and a great father, and I knew all men weren’t monsters, I’d still heard so many horror stories over the hotline. Yet there I was.

I’m not sure whether my story opened the floodgates, the way hotline calls had for us volunteers. Or maybe they wanted to assure me of my safety. But, slowly, several of the men started resharing their own stories, going deeper. One said he’d been raped as a child and wanted to hurt people like he’d been hurt. One admitted he’d not only raped a woman but also sodomized her with a broom handle, killing her. That’s the story that shook me to my core; I still shake today from that dreadful image.

Finally, one of the men said he raped other men who were incarcerated with him. After that confession, the room went silent. As I looked around the room, I suspected all of them had done the same, or been victims themselves. Lorton warehoused teen boys as well as adult men, and the stronger preyed on the weaker. Since all of them had entered Lorton as teenagers, they continued the cycle until they chose not to.

That’s when I felt their desperation. I didn’t question why hearing the sordid details of the violence they’d inflicted somehow made me feel safer. Go figure. I can only surmise they admitted their crimes to build trust. No one was forcing them to be there. They came because they wanted to change, and I felt that. They ached to reclaim their humanity. Incarcerated people are rarely listened to. Society does not want to hear what happened to them, only what they’ve done to others. Prisoners Against Rape was their way of lifting their voices as victimized violators, to bring attention to their pain as well as their crimes.

So began my weekly journeys to Lorton, a tradition that lasted three transformative years. I looked forward to Fridays as the capstone of my week. Working with the prisoners in what became an informal book club provided an intellectually stimulating break from the stress of maintaining a barely viable nonprofit organization. I felt I spent too much time seeking grants and donations, managing staff and budgets, negotiating with police and hospitals about how victims were treated, and representing the organization to politicians, donors, and the media. As the executive director, I was removed from the more fulfilling day-to-day counseling services. Going to Lorton began to recharge my enthusiasm for the work and reinforced my optimism that we could make a difference—that we could prevent sexual assaults.

Sometimes others from the Center joined me on visits, like Nkenge Touré or Yulanda Ward. They admitted that they didn’t go on that first trip because they wanted to see if I came out safe. When I told them what happened, they shared my fascination with this informal experiment: Could we convince men not to rape? We had no special training or insight, but we and the prisoners bonded as fellow survivors. We witnessed their pain as well as their joy. They had grown from boys to men in prison, and now they were adults experiencing regrets and seeking better futures. Many of those Fridays were filled with as much laughter as learning.

They learned to listen to women’s voices; I learned to widen my perception of men who rape. These men I saw each Friday seemed like a brotherhood, supporting one another emotionally and perhaps physically as well. If one person had a pack of cigarettes, they shared instead of hoarding them. While I could never fathom all they’d experienced, I could see their excitement at learning new things together as we lifted each other’s consciousness and explored Black feminist theory. I didn’t know who we were until we discovered what we could do together. The work built the relationships instead of the relationships building the work. We stumbled, sometimes harming each other, but we learned to move beyond our comfort zones together.

Soon, I was invited to join them on Family Visitor Day. It was like a Fourth of July celebration without fireworks, hosted on the large grass-covered quad in the center of the minimum-security area. The tables were covered with white tablecloths and filled with food. Wives, girlfriends, children, and other family and friends arrived in their most festive summer outfits to share brief moments with their loved ones behind bars. It was a chance for them to feel like they could live normal lives, with laughter and tears affirming relationships that endured under the most constricted of circumstances.

When strange things happened during these family visits, the men quickly encircled me to protect me from noticing something a little off; I was warned never to look under a tablecloth that was rapidly moving with no wind. The skirted tables were their “bedrooms.” I only later realized the irony of a rape survivor being surrounded by six rapists and feeling protected. They treated me as a smart little sister whom they had to respect and guard at the same time.

I’m not certain when the emotional shift in my relationship with the men occurred. But as I delighted in the joy of shared learning and mutual growth, I forgot to be angry and afraid. I learned that my anger at them was largely born of fear, and eliminating the fear dissipated the anger. Perhaps I was able to relate to the men in Lorton because, when I first passed through those gates, I’d felt caged within myself. Maybe it was because I’d been caged at that home for unwed mothers.

Whatever the cause, I discovered a capacity for trust under the most improbable of circumstances. I learned to see the humanity of those I had only previously seen as predators. They had seemed nothing but evil to me until they helped me see them in all their complexities, with all their challenges and contradictions. By the end of our sessions, I appreciated and respected them as co-conspirators working to end rape.

Improbability is pointed. Since it would deeply hurt to be in presence with those who have done exactly what is unfathomably horrific—not just hurt but be both uncomfortable and unraveling. Rationally, it would be comfortable to live out a live separate from the ones that we call abusers, aggressors, oppressors, the ones who wage war and the ones who represent us in the government.

Though, even if one is in their presence, working alongside them or even as a resisting protestor, resistance often re-embodies the same distress that is occuring. Which enforces the separation between people who are very in touch with their needs.

One of my teachers in Holocaust and Genocide studies expressed that, often, in our desperation to understood, we forget to be understanding. So, if we call out all those who violate our sense of justice, then we forget to understand that "calling out" exactly re-enacts the massacre. Almost in this rhyming way because it is as if the division further divides.

Something that connects to this is in Kazu Haga’s Fierce Vulnerability. In it, he expressed how activism, even nonviolence, can sometimes activate violence. Yet not even just activate but amplify it. He questioned the approach of what an “appropriate response” looks like because he had experienced this first-hand, of how activist groups and social justice movements could re-embody the same crisis they sought to fix and heal:

So, what could an appropriate response to an overt social conflict look like?

Nonviolent responses to over conflicts often look like shutting down a highway, occupying a government building, or organizing a general strike. Escalated responses to conflict are, by nature, disruptive. But one thing I have noticed is oftentimes, the more we escalate our tactics of nonviolent action, the more we escalate polarization: a black/white, us/them, right/wrong world view of division and separation. And remember, a black-white binary perspective can be a sign of a panic zone—”good” side, and they become “bad.” We’re here to shut them down and defeat them so we can win and achieve justice.

Unfortunately, I believe the black/white, us/them worldview is at the heart of what is destroying our planet. Many psychologists say separation and isolation are at the core of trauma. It’s our inability to remember our interdependence that allows us to cause harm to each other. I don’t believe we can heal our wounds by continuing to perpetuate this binary worldview of separation.

In organizing circles, we’re often taught to bring injustice to the >surface and force people to pick sides, essentially polarizing the public. But there’s a big difference in asking people to choose between justice and injustice and asking them to choose between us and them, thereby forgoing our interdependence to one another. We will never get to liberation if we build movements that may be able to win short-term gains on various issues but move us further away from each other on a relational level.

Since justice is in the weeds, and, in due course, a disambiguation of weeds is necessary. One needs to reclaim the 'weeds.'

What is commmonly not known is how that many species of fauna and flora considered to be invasive species are actually edible. Yet, not many people know this or even eat them if they know. So. how does one metabolize a weed? Not just in knowing it but of digesting it?

PRACTICE

I am sensitive to how much of my writing lacks reference practice—if I write this, give no provocation outside of text itself, I could very well be hiding in ambiguation. As with any suggestion of practice that gives name to the trickster, I invite you to do the opposite of what I write. Or not even the opposite but anything else. Because I have been hurt deeply by people telling me what I need to do and need to know. So, here is a practice:

Weeding justice

I would like you to conceive of your deepest wishes for how you would like things to be like. Perhaps, the way you want others to treat you, the way you want life to meet you where you are or the way you wish you could have reorganized your past knowing and experiencing all that you have now.

Now, I want to remind you, uncooked, weeding justice is a bitter medicine. So, this practice may be challenging to swallow yet not so much digest. To truly digest how justice could be re-incarceration, for those, like me, who only learn through negative wisdom or learning by doing what not to do. Receiving the teachings in doing exactly that which will give the most clear message, in which a clarity is often found in attraction to the total obliquity and obscuring of life opaque. Or more simply put: life not seen clearly. I want you to consider something even if just briefly.

Something Michael Ventura observed was this:

You are not one person, you are many people, you are a community of moods and selves under one name. Part of you aren't even human, they're part mammal, part reptile, part rose, part moon, part wind. And life is a question of which parts are dominant—which, in effect, possess you. (I think most people walk around possessed by the dullest parts of themselves; this, the worst state of possesion, is called "normal.")

If one considers this, it would be of alarm because more often than not, we live in remediation from the abnormal. We want to be normal and that people who deviate are out of standard. This is the door that typified science or rationality hinges upon. Things have an order that is to be maintained by staying close to normal.

Now, normal is not really the issue at all because justice, again, is in the weeds. I want you to consider something rather ordinary:

Body hair, the way you are around others (those you respect and those you do not), all your disappointments hanging upon you like clothes, and the intense curiosities that lays dormant and perhaps even extinct. Off of those things, I want you to touch into things that connects to those things. And reflect in that.

Reflect in that in this way: how does normal restrict the range of your body's expression? How much does normal become synonyms with tame, quell, pacify, postpone, and conceal the beauty that we know ourselves to be if our needs of full expression beyond dualities of normal and abnromal could ever touch?

More directly: what are the things you name yourself as abnormal or other from yourself that you want to eradicate from yourself or others?

Especially of others if you have more sensitivity to them and especially of yourself because everything Other reflects in You. Because there is something wildly simple that weeding justice roots within:

Where you most resent, you need the most love.

Often, what we resent is "put off" as "not for us." Or what we believe is exactly other to ourself. Which is exactly contribute to how justice is in the weeds.

Just to tell you about a rooted crisis, there are things called "invasive species" . Yet, what scientists and researchers are catching up to is the wisdom of the weed. Or the wisdom of the foreign

. Foreign meaning the people far away from us. Not only in physical distance but of those we feel most distant to or isolated from.

The initiation to metabolizing a weed is to decompose the word "invasive." What if the things we want far away from us only makes them more present? What if justice is not in weeding things but uprooting and uncovering the displacement within ourselves?

Something that has emerged recently (1904-present) emerges from Frederic E. Clements' ecotone which essentially expressed the most fertile ground of connection and contact is found in the point of transition or "boundary" between two wildly distinct biomes. Of places of trans-iton or in-between. Though not simply liminal spaces but of liminality in reference to two places of divergence that expose a deeper connection if they are in contact and conversation.

Could you explore the ecotone? There may be something waiting for you there...
